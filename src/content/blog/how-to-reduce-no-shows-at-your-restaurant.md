A no-show is one of the most frustrating ways to lose money in hospitality. You've held the table, ordered the stock, rostered the staff — and at 7:30 the chairs sit empty. Unlike a quiet night, you planned for these covers, so the loss stings twice. The good news: a few simple changes reliably bring the rate down.

## Why guests don't turn up

Most no-shows aren't malicious. They're forgetful, double-booked, or they tried to cancel and couldn't get through. That last one matters more than owners realise: **a guest who can't reach you to cancel becomes a no-show by default** — and the table you could have re-sold stays empty because you never knew it was free.

So the problem isn't only the guest. It's often the gap in how bookings are captured and confirmed.

## The tactics that actually work

- **Confirm every booking.** A short reminder the day before — by SMS or a quick call — cuts no-shows sharply. It nudges the forgetful and flushes out the double-booked while you can still re-sell the table.
- **Make cancelling effortless.** Counter-intuitive, but true: the easier you make it to cancel, the more notice you get, and the more tables you recover. A guest who can cancel in ten seconds will; one who has to call a phone that rings out simply won't show.
- **Take a deposit for large groups.** For bookings of six or more, or peak slots, a small per-head deposit dramatically improves turn-up rates. Most diners expect it now.
- **Capture the booking properly in the first place.** Wrong night, wrong number, "I thought I cancelled" — a lot of no-shows are really booking errors. Getting the details right at the point of booking prevents them.

## Where the phone fits in

Two of those four come back to one thing: **answering the phone, every time.** If callers can always reach you, they can always cancel — and you can always confirm.
